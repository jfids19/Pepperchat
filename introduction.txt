You might think robots, artificial intelligence, computers and cybersecurity are all
very modern ideas. But one of the problems computers solve today is actually
thousands of years old.

How can you send somebody a message without everybody else being able
to understand it?

Imagine you need to send an important message to another group in this room.
You write it down and somebody intercepts it on the way. If they can simply read
the message, your secret is gone.

So what could you do?

More than 2,000 years ago, Julius Caesar is associated with a simple method of
disguising messages. Instead of writing the real letters, each letter is moved - or
shifted - along the alphabet.

If we use a shift of three, A becomes D, B becomes E, and C becomes F.
That means the word HELLO becomes KHOOR.

To somebody who does not know the rule, KHOOR might look meaningless. But if
you know the key - shift every letter back three places - you can recover the
original message.

First, Jack is going to give you encrypted messages to crack. Then you will have
to encrypt a message yourselves.

But your final mission is more interesting.

Each team will create its own secret message, choose its own Caesar shift,
encrypt it, and pass the ciphertext to another team. That team will have to try to
break your code.

So by the end of the workshop, you will have been both the cryptographer
protecting information and the codebreaker trying to uncover it.

And while a Caesar cipher is fun, there is one important problem: there are not
many possible keys. A computer can test every Caesar shift almost instantly.

Modern encryption is enormously more sophisticated, but the basic idea you are
exploring today still matters: take readable information, transform it using a
method and a key, and make it difficult for an unauthorised person to
understand.

So, codebreakers: your mission is to protect your messages, intercept the other
teams' messages, and see who can crack the codes.
